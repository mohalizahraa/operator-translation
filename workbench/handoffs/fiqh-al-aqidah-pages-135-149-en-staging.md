# Evidence for the Permissibility of Taqlīd in Theology

The position that taqlīd is impermissible in theological matters has become widespread among leading Muslim scholars, many of whom have presented different arguments to establish their claim. Others have merely transmitted the prohibition in a form resembling a fatwa and claimed consensus upon it. Al-ʿAllāmah al-Ḥillī ﵋ says in this regard: «All scholars unanimously agree that knowledge of Allah ﷻ, His ﷻ affirmative and negative attributes, what is possible for Him ﷻ and what is impossible for Him ﷻ, prophethood, Imamate, and the Return must be attained through evidence rather than taqlīd.» [[S135-1]]

Likewise, the author of Al-ʿUrwah confines the sphere of taqlīd to legal rulings and excludes the principles of religion, saying: «The domain and subject matter of taqlīd is the practical subsidiary rulings; it does not apply to the principles of religion…» [[S135-2]]

The apparent meaning of their words is taqlīd in the jurisprudential sense, by which they mean the nonexpert referring to the expert. At that time there had been no established precedent for distinguishing the levels of taqlīd, nor were the true intended meanings of taqlīd in theological matters clearly articulated. Later scholars therefore inherited the position from earlier ones as though it were among the settled assumptions, without pausing over it in a way that would remove the points of difficulty surrounding the claim concerning taqlīd.

If we examine the two preceding statements and the many similar statements of leading scholars, we find that they revolve around prohibiting taqlīd in the foundational headings themselves among the principles of religion. There is no dispute about this: no one maintains the permissibility of taqlīd in these five headings according to the school of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵊, or the three headings according to the school of the Companions.

This may be the first point to which attention should have been drawn: a distinction must be made between the principles of religion as foundational headings and the questions that fall under those headings. We maintain that taqlīd of the fatwa is permissible in the overwhelming majority of those subsidiary questions, not universally. Some questions permit only the second form, taqlīd of the evidence, quite apart from the foundational headings themselves that the famous theologians call the principles of religion and which, in our view, are confined to divine unity, prophethood, and the Return.

The very claim of consensus advanced among the proofs for prohibiting taqlīd in theological matters may itself support the conclusion that what they intended was prohibition only in the foundational headings rather than in every theological question. There are clearly multiple dissenters from the broader claim whose contrary views cannot legitimately be ignored when consensus is alleged, among them Shaykh al-Ṭāʾifah al-Ṭūsī ﵋. We shall examine the claim of consensus and its problems shortly.

Likewise, the statement of one contemporary scholar that «they obligated knowledge of the principles of religion and did not permit taqlīd in them because the route to acquiring knowledge of them is easy and available to every person under religious obligation» [[S136-1]] is appropriate only if confined to the foundational headings of theology—divine unity, prophethood, and the Return. The questions branching from each principle are not easy for every person under religious obligation, because they require investigation and reasoning that are available only to one who has reached the level of derivation.

Objectivity would therefore have required the matter to be presented with a distinction between the position concerning the foundational headings of theology and the questions contained beneath those headings. In order to treat the opposing position in the strongest and most objective form, however, we shall assume that those who prohibit taqlīd intend a universal rule encompassing all theological questions. To clarify the true position, we must present their most important arguments for prohibiting taqlīd in theology and discuss them, ultimately arriving at what is required by human innate disposition, the rational principle, and rational human practice: the necessity of the nonexpert referring to the expert.

## First Argument: Consensus

We previously cited the statement of al-ʿAllāmah al-Ḥillī ﵋ claiming consensus that ijtihād and the establishment of evidence are necessary in theology and that taqlīd is prohibited. This claimed consensus is said to encompass both major schools. Al-Qurṭubī writes: «Seventh: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah said: the community has unanimously agreed upon the invalidity of taqlīd in theological beliefs.» [[S137-1]]

In the same connection, the author of Al-Mustamsak says: «Indeed, consensus of the Muslims has been claimed upon it.» [[S137-2]] The claimed consensus of the community is therefore not confined to scholars of the school of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵊ but is said to encompass Muslim scholars generally. The claim, however, can be challenged from several directions.

First: consensus is authoritative only where we neither know nor reasonably suspect that it rests upon some independent proof. Here such a basis is plainly possible—for example, the arguments they themselves present for the impermissibility of taqlīd. We must therefore return to those other proofs and examine whether they are complete.

Second: what we have actually observed regarding the prohibition of taqlīd in theology is widespread fame, not consensus. A number of leading scholars themselves confirm this, including the author of Al-Mustamsak, who writes of «the discussion concerning whether knowledge must arise from reflection and evidence—as is attributed to one group—or whether this is not obligatory—as is attributed to others—or whether such reflection is prohibited—as is attributed to still others…» [[S138-1]] He ﵋ then says: «The more apparent view is the first where abandoning inquiry creates fear of misguidance; the last where inquiry creates fear of misguidance; and the second where one is secure from misguidance whether one investigates or does not. Review and reflect.» [[S138-2]]

The author of Al-Mustamsak thus distinguishes among cases. If a person would go astray by failing to investigate, he makes investigation obligatory; if investigation itself would lead him astray, he makes it prohibited; if he is safe from misguidance whether through independent inquiry or through taqlīd, he may adopt either route. The criterion is avoiding misguidance. This itself demonstrates that he does not accept the alleged consensus.

There is therefore no consensus establishing the prohibition of taqlīd in theological matters. Referring to a qualified religious authority specializing in theology is sufficient when it produces knowledge or reassurance, because the criterion is the attainment of knowledge or reassurance, whether that state is reached through one’s own effort or through the effort of another.

It should also be noted that the common expression in the writings of many leading scholars that taqlīd in theological matters is “prohibited” does not ordinarily mean a legislative prohibition whose violation is itself a sin. Rather, what is intended is a situational invalidity: on that view, even if a person acquires knowledge or reassurance concerning a theological belief through taqlīd, this route does not suffice to validate his belief. It does not mean that by doing so he has committed a forbidden act deserving punishment. A legislative prohibition entails sin and liability to punishment; a situational prohibition entails the invalidity of the act or belief.

We likewise accept such situational invalidity in theology—but only in those matters where taqlīd is impermissible. As has become clear, there are questions in which taqlīd is absolutely impermissible; questions in which taqlīd is permissible at the level of the evidence; and questions in which taqlīd is permissible at the level of the fatwa. Wherever taqlīd of the fatwa is valid, taqlīd of the evidence is also valid, but the converse does not follow.

We also maintain that the validity and acceptance of deeds, or their invalidity and nonacceptance, are consequent upon the soundness or unsoundness of theology. A number of effects follow from theological principles, and among their most important effects is the validation and acceptance of deeds. Whoever wishes his deeds to be accepted must therefore correct his beliefs first and then proceed to action. The Noble Qurʾān points to this when it joins faith to righteous action: ﴾ Those who believe and do righteous deeds… ﴿ [[Q135-1]] If faith contains a defect, the deed has no value. Perhaps this is the reason the famous terminology calls these beliefs “the principles of religion”: they constitute the foundation and root of practical matters.

## Second Argument: The Qurʾānic Emphasis on Reflection and Inquiry

Among the proofs advanced by those who prohibit taqlīd in theology is the Qurʾān. They appeal to numerous verses that strongly emphasize reflection and inquiry and appear to direct people toward acquiring knowledge by evidence and demonstration rather than by another route. Allah ﷻ says: ﴾ So relate the accounts, so that perhaps they may reflect. ﴿ [[Q140-1]] He ﷻ says: ﴾ Thus do We ﷻ explain the signs in detail for a people who reflect. ﴿ [[Q140-2]] He ﷻ says: ﴾ Surely in that are signs for a people who reflect. ﴿ [[Q140-3]] And He ﷻ says: ﴾ We ﷻ sent down to you ﷺ the Reminder so that you ﷺ may make clear to the people what was sent down to them, and so that perhaps they may reflect. ﴿ [[Q140-4]] There are dozens of other verses speaking of the necessity of reflection, contemplation, inquiry, and reasoning, which stand opposite imitation and mere following.

**Discussion:** the most these verses establish is guidance toward routes by which knowledge or reassurance may be acquired, among them reflection, reasoning, and inquiry. They do not negate other routes. Put differently, they establish their own subject—reflection—and it is obvious that establishing one route does not negate every other route.

It is entirely correct to say that what these verses establish is the best route to religious truths and fields of knowledge. Reflection and contemplation in understanding and reaching truths take precedence over other methods. There is therefore no contradiction between these verses and referring to a specialist in theological matters. We have always affirmed, and continue to affirm, the superiority of inquiry and reflection in acquiring theological belief. But this highest route cannot be imposed upon every person under religious obligation in every theological question, for that would amount to imposing what lies beyond capacity. Whoever is able to pursue it possesses a tremendous good. Whoever cannot—and this is the condition of the overwhelming majority of persons under religious obligation—is not left in ignorance but is required to refer to a specialist authority in accordance with the innate and rational principle of the nonexpert referring to the expert.

The Noble Qurʾān therefore calls us to reflection and contemplation, and one instance of that guidance is its direction that we refer to people of knowledge and specialization regarding what we do not know. It states explicitly: ﴾ Ask the People of the Reminder if you do not know. ﴿ [[Q140-5]] From this we understand recourse to people of knowledge and remembrance in all religious fields.

## No Necessary Connection Between the Proofs Requiring Inquiry and the Prohibition of Taqlīd

Moreover, none of the Qurʾānic and transmitted proofs advanced—as will also be seen below—for the obligation of inquiry and knowledge necessarily entails prohibiting taqlīd in theological matters. Sayyid Muḥsin al-Ḥakīm ﵋ takes this position in Al-Mustamsak, saying: «As for what indicates the obligation of knowledge and recognition from the verses and reports, this does not entail that [taqlīd is impermissible], because evidence establishing the authority of another’s considered opinion—if established—would establish knowledge through it by way of legally constructed equivalence…» [[S141-1]] That is, theological knowledge obtained through the verses and reports may be legally treated as knowledge through authoritative reliance, rather than arising from a person’s own rational ijtihād and inquiry.

## Third Argument: Qurʾānic Verses Condemning Taqlīd

A number of Qurʾānic verses condemning taqlīd have been advanced here. Allah ﷻ says: ﴾ When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah ﷻ has sent down,” they say, “Rather, we follow what we found our fathers upon.” Even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided? ﴿ [[Q141-1]] He ﷻ says: ﴾ When it is said to them, “Come to what Allah ﷻ has sent down and to the Messenger ﷺ,” they say, “Sufficient for us is what we found our fathers upon.” Even if their fathers knew nothing and were not guided? ﴿ [[Q141-2]] He ﷻ says: ﴾ We found our fathers upon a way, and we are guided by following their footsteps. ﴿ [[Q141-3]] And He ﷻ says: ﴾ Likewise, We ﷻ sent no warner before you ﷺ to any town without its affluent people saying, “We found our fathers upon a way, and we follow their footsteps.” ﴿ [[Q141-4]] Other related verses likewise condemn taqlīd.

There is no doubt that these verses address people who stood against the message of the prophets by clinging to imitation of their fathers and predecessors. Some have attempted to infer from them that taqlīd in theological matters is condemned because it is the certain instance of the taqlīd being censured, while recourse to specialists in practical matters is excluded because independent evidence establishes its permissibility, including rational human practice and a number of reports, among them: «They are My proof over you, and I am the proof of Allah ﷻ.» [[S142-1]]

**Discussion:** there is no doubt concerning the validity of the nonexpert referring to the expert. This is required by reason, rational human practice, and the religious texts. The condemned form of recourse is specifically the ignorant person referring to another ignorant person. The verses above reject precisely this unjustified form of imitation. An ignorant person lacks knowledge; how can one refer to him concerning what he does not know? As the familiar maxim states, one who lacks a thing cannot give it. By contrast, the nonexpert referring to the expert—including the person ignorant of theology referring to one who knows theology—is praiseworthy and required. As long as the person under religious obligation does not himself know, he must refer to one who does.

Those whose imitation the Qurʾān condemns were condemned because they referred to people as ignorant as themselves. In summary, although the Noble Qurʾān condemns taqlīd through these verses, it intends a particular form of taqlīd, not taqlīd without qualification. There is condemned taqlīd and there is praiseworthy taqlīd. Condemned taqlīd is that which rests on no prior rationally grounded criterion establishing that the person consulted is in fact qualified to be consulted. Recourse to an unqualified person in his supposed field of knowledge is invalid and insufficient. The condemnation therefore attaches to taqlīd that lacks the rational, customary-rational, and textual criterion—namely, the nonexpert referring to the expert.

The Noble Qurʾān itself presents and emphasizes this truth within the same verses. It says: ﴾ When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah ﷻ has sent down,” they say, “Rather, we follow what we found our fathers upon”… ﴿ [[Q143-1]] The Qurʾānic argument confronts their argument that they follow what they found their fathers upon, and the same verse immediately explains why such recourse is unsound: ﴾ Even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided? ﴿ [[Q143-2]] The condemnation is therefore specifically directed at an ignorant person referring to another ignorant person. The problem is not that they refer to their fathers simply because they are their fathers; it is that those fathers are neither knowledgeable nor guided. From the verse we can therefore infer the permissibility of recourse when the person consulted is rational, knowledgeable, and guided. The same applies to the previously cited verse 5:104, which describes the fathers to whom they refer as lacking knowledge.

Recourse to an unqualified person is obviously condemned even outside religion. A sick person must consult a specialist—a physician—not simply any person; otherwise his recourse is blameworthy. His consultation of a specialist, by contrast, is praiseworthy and necessary unless he is himself a physician.

The Noble Qurʾān has therefore divided taqlīd into two kinds: condemned taqlīd, which is recourse to one who lacks specialization, and praiseworthy taqlīd, which is recourse to the specialist in his field.

## Fourth Argument: Taqlīd Produces Conjecture, Which Is Insufficient in Theology

They have argued that taqlīd produces nothing beyond conjecture, and that conjecture is insufficient in theological matters because it does not produce belief in the required sense—namely, conviction for which certainty must be attained. Taqlīd is therefore invalid in theology and, according to this argument, prohibited as well.

**Discussion:** as we have repeatedly indicated, in those theological questions where taqlīd is valid we require the attainment of reassurance. Permissible taqlīd is therefore conditioned rather than unrestricted. The objection applies only against a view that permits taqlīd without such a condition.

The background of this objection likely returns to one of two assumptions. The first is the belief that those who permit taqlīd in theology do not exclude the foundational questions; the correct position on that has already become clear. The second is the assumption that taqlīd produces nothing but conjecture. We have already explained that there is a higher level of taqlīd capable of producing reassurance. Any theological taqlīd that fails to produce reassurance is invalid.

Another possible source of the objection is rejection of solitary reports as a basis for accepting theological claims. This is correct where a solitary report produces only conjecture. If it produces reassurance, however, depending upon the nature of the report and the scholarly-religious identity of the reporter, there is no problem in accepting it. The criterion lies in the result—reassurance—not in the particular route by which it was reached. If a person under religious obligation believes something and his heart becomes settled upon it through a solitary report, a report approaching knowledge, a mutawātir report, spiritual disclosure, or a spiritual state that leads him to that conviction, what he believes is acceptable for him. [[S144-1]] We shall later discuss the authority of solitary reports in theological questions.

## The Indication of the Verse of Tafaqquh for Taqlīd in Theology

Allah ﷻ says: ﴾ It is not for all the believers to go forth together. Rather, why should a group from every division of them not go forth to gain a deep understanding of religion and warn their people when they return to them, so that they may beware? ﴿ [[Q145-1]]

This noble verse has been used as evidence for three matters:

1. The authority of a trustworthy solitary report.
2. The necessity of ijtihād in practical matters called legal rulings.
3. The permissibility of taqlīd in legal rulings.

The third argument is what concerns us here. We have no dispute concerning the necessity of ijtihād in theological matters, specifically the foundational headings conventionally called the principles of religion. Nor do we dispute acceptance of a trustworthy report in legal matters. In theological questions, however, the general criterion is the attainment of reassurance. [[S146-1]] If a solitary report produces reassurance, it may be accepted; otherwise it may not.

Ordinarily, a solitary report requires supporting considerations to elevate its evidential disclosure to the level of reassurance. Among those considerations is the scholarly and moral identity of the reporter. A narrator of the highest degree of justice and trustworthiness is not equivalent to an ordinary trustworthy narrator. The epistemic effect produced by a report transmitted by Zurārah ibn Aʿyan, Muḥammad ibn Muslim, Ṣafwān ibn Yaḥyā, Muḥammad ibn Abī ʿUmayr, Yūnus ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, and those at their level of probity and reliability differs from that produced by other narrators even when those others are just or trustworthy.

As for the permissibility of taqlīd in legal rulings on the basis of this noble verse—which follows from the command to warn and from recourse to those who possess understanding of religion—if the verse is a valid proof for taqlīd in legal rulings, the same rationale makes it a valid proof for taqlīd in theology. As explained earlier, [[S146-2]] religion is not confined to legal rulings but centrally encompasses theological questions. Indeed, theology is the root and foundation of religion, while everything else follows it in a vertical order. The language of the verse is plainly not restricted to legal rulings. Its indication of the necessity of gaining deep understanding—in the sense of ijtihād—and of the permissibility of taqlīd in theological matters is therefore even more evident, given the priority of theology over the Sharīʿah and its status as the root and foundation of religion.

From here it can be said that every proof used to establish the obligation of ijtihād in practical matters can likewise be used to establish the obligation of ijtihād in theological matters, and every proof used to establish the permissibility of taqlīd in the Sharīʿah is itself evidence for the permissibility of taqlīd in theology so long as the language of the proof speaks about religion generally. Thus, when the verse says “to gain a deep understanding of religion,” religion—as has become clear—is broader than practical matters; it encompasses both practical and theological matters.

Further supporting the verse’s indication of both ijtihād and taqlīd, as a number of leading scholars have understood, is that some of its expressions—such as “that they may warn” and “that they may beware”—are more naturally indicative of the obligation of ijtihād in theology than in practical matters. In our view, they are likewise more naturally indicative of the permissibility of taqlīd in theology than in practical matters.

Actual lived reality may be the clearest witness to this. If we survey theological questions, we do not find all of them to be settled matters; many are disputed. This necessarily requires specialists able to provide answers to such disputed questions, and consequently requires recourse to those specialists, because not every person under religious obligation can attain specialist expertise in theology, just as not everyone can specialize in every other technical discipline.

The same rationale applies to the words of Allah ﷻ: ﴾ Ask the People of the Reminder if you do not know. ﴿ [[Q147-1]] The verse clearly indicates the obligation to ask in a state of ignorance. The implication of asking is that one acts in accordance with the answer; the primary purpose cannot be asking for its own sake, since a question producing no practical effect would be pointless and there would be no justification for commanding it. [[S147-1]]

The verse therefore indicates the permissibility of taqlīd and the authority of the knowledgeable person’s fatwa for the ignorant person. This is required by the principle of the nonexpert referring to the expert. “The People of the Reminder” means people of knowledge and is not restricted to the People of the Book. As Sayyid al-Khūʾī ﵋ explains: «The noble verse contains a universal major premise that may apply to the People of the Book, may apply to the Imams ﵊, and may apply to the scholar and jurist, according to the different contextual relationships required by different situations.» [[S148-1]]

## The Role of Self-Evidence and Clarity in the Permissibility and Impermissibility of Taqlīd

If we consider legal questions as a whole, we find that some have reached the level of religious necessity, such as the basic obligation of prayer, fasting, ḥajj, and zakāt, while others have reached the level of certainty. Wherever a matter reaches this level, taqlīd is impermissible. This is a general rule upon which there is consensus. [[S148-2]] A necessary or certain proposition has no higher epistemic level to which it could be elevated by substituting the statement of a mujtahid, which produces at most probability or, in the best case, reassurance for the follower. There is no sense in attaching what is authoritative through another to what is authoritative in itself.

Indeed, it is meaningless to speak of a mujtahid having an “opinion” in matters of necessity and certainty, or of the process of ijtihād having any function there. As our teacher Sayyid al-Khūʾī ﵋ states: «Establishing such matters does not depend upon the aforementioned process, so there is no subject for taqlīd in them. Taqlīd is following the mujtahid in his considered opinion, and he has no considered opinion in these matters, because their relation to the layperson and to others is the same. The same applies to purely external subjects, where there is no scope for derivation.» [[S148-3]]

As for a person who fails to attain certainty concerning what is certain in itself, or for whom something necessary in itself has not reached the degree of necessity, his denial is not given weight, because he is either obsessively doubtful or otherwise epistemically unsound.

Matters that reach the level of necessity, self-evidence, or certainty are therefore already known to the person under religious obligation; there is no meaning in exercising ijtihād concerning them, much less taqlīd. From this one can understand our position in theology: the certain minimum of theological matters that reach the level of necessity, self-evidence, or certainty for everyone consists precisely in the foundational headings called, in the famous terminology, the principles of religion. Other questions are, in the overwhelming majority, theoretical matters in which one who is capable must exercise ijtihād and one who cannot attain the rank of ijtihād may practice taqlīd.

This is a general rule concerning necessary, self-evident, and certain matters: taqlīd is never permissible in them, whether they concern legal rulings or theology. The converse rule is also general. In theoretical matters, a person must exercise ijtihād to the extent of his capacity or resort to taqlīd in accordance with rational human practice, founded upon the nonexpert referring to the expert. This rule is not restricted to one field rather than another. It is therefore meaningless to say without qualification, “Taqlīd is impermissible in theology.” The matter instead requires distinction.

Theological questions are obviously no different in this respect from questions in other sciences, where a human being is responsible only for what lies within his capacity, in accordance with the Qurʾānic rule. [[S149-1]] If the person under religious obligation is capable of acquiring knowledge and reaching the level of ijtihād in theology, he must do so. Otherwise, he is excused. Since he cannot remain without sound theology, he must refer to a specialist and adopt from him through taqlīd those questions in which taqlīd is permissible, as explained above.

[[S149-1]] This rule is derived from the words of Allah ﷻ: ﴾ Allah ﷻ does not burden a soul beyond its capacity. ﴿ (Qurʾān 2:286). Its roots are rational and grounded in rational human practice: sound reason judges it impossible to impose what lies beyond capacity. There is therefore no sense in requiring all persons under religious obligation to exercise ijtihād in every theological question, which is no smaller in number or depth than the questions of the Sharīʿah. Such a demand would be exceedingly difficult—indeed, ordinarily impossible—even for students of religious learning who have not reached the level of ijtihād, so how much more so for others. If we ruled that ijtihād is necessary in every theological question, the implication would be that the generality of people who have not reached ijtihād are sinners or unbelievers—Allah ﷻ forbid—which is manifestly false. No option therefore remains except permitting taqlīd outside the three principal theological headings—divine unity, prophethood, and the Return—or, put differently, outside matters established by innate disposition.

In summary, the person under religious obligation must exert his capacity to gain a deep understanding of the affairs of his religion in both theology and the Sharīʿah. Wherever he is capable of ijtihād, taqlīd is prohibited for him. Wherever he is incapable, taqlīd is permissible except in matters that are innate, necessary, or certain, whether in the Sharīʿah or theology. In those areas where taqlīd is permissible, the rank of taqlīd in theology differs from taqlīd in the Sharīʿah only in the necessity of attaining at least reassurance when acting upon a theological fatwa.
