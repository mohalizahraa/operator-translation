Some contemporary scholars have said: «There is no objection to permitting a person who is incapable of independent inquiry to suffice with taqlīd; otherwise, this would entail imposing the impossible. To claim that no such incapable person exists is sheer obstinacy in the face of what we directly observe: the intellects of many people are unable to comprehend even the simplest forms of evidence, so how could they grasp the subtleties of demonstration whose comprehension can elude even people of learning and knowledge?» [[S131-1]]

Many of our contemporary leading scholars have recognized this point and, in practice, permitted taqlīd in theological matters. They formulated that permissibility through a technically sophisticated expression intended to avoid explicitly saying that taqlīd in theology is permissible. After obligating people to exercise ijtihād in theology and prohibiting taqlīd in it, they recognized that people were incapable of performing the role of independent theological inquiry. They therefore offered a justificatory formulation that implicitly permits taqlīd in theology. We can observe this carefully in the words of one eminent scholar when answering a question about ijtihād in theology and the impermissibility of taqlīd: «The meaning of not practicing taqlīd is not that you simply adopt whatever conclusion your own reflection reaches. Rather, belief and certainty must rest upon a sound basis, even if that basis is general—for example, knowing that all the jurists and deeply learned scholars agree upon a particular theological matter, and that if it were not true they would not have agreed upon it. This is called general evidence for the correctness of belief in that proposition.» [[S131-2]]

The evidence of the person under religious obligation for a theological conclusion is therefore not necessarily the detailed proof that he himself must personally master. It can be sufficient for him to know that the scholars agree upon the question, since following them in that respect is valid.

Accordingly, some explicitly state that if a person capable of inquiry abandons it, yet attains certainty through taqlīd and his belief corresponds to reality, there is no objection to his becoming a believer and a Muslim and to the legal effects of both statuses applying to him. Certainty and conviction acquired through taqlīd may in fact be stronger than certainty acquired through demonstration. Yes, by opposing the judgment of his reason that inquiry is obligatory, he commits tajarrī. If one holds that tajarrī merits punishment, then punishment follows; otherwise, there is no punishment at all. [[S132-1]]

If we surveyed the statements of most of our contemporary leading scholars in this field, whether written or spoken, we would find them revolving around the permissibility of taqlīd in theology in one form or another—forms that amount to a call for theological authority without saying so explicitly. Hardly any of their websites is free of direct answers to theological questions without presenting the evidence for those answers; such answers therefore amount to theological fatwas. It is likely that they strongly incline toward permitting taqlīd in theology, but are prevented from stating this explicitly by the force of an inherited consensus accepted through imitation and by a famous opinion that has no foundation here. What distinguishes us from them is that we have stated openly what their consciences have concealed. We have assumed responsibility for releasing the community from an obligation beyond its capacity and rescuing it from the confusion and disorder from which even students of religious learning are not spared, let alone others. Just as we are confident in the correctness of the course we have adopted and in our call for a sound religious authority capable of carrying the community's burdens in all the fields of religious knowledge it needs, we are equally confident that this epistemic and practical call will advance and succeed in its mission, and that many of the community's best scholars, leading figures, students, and preachers will join this blessed course out of their religious and historical responsibility to defend Islam, knowledge, understanding, and action in a manner responsive to the requirements of the age.

## The Scope of Taqlīd in Theology

The preceding discussion showed that there are three levels of taqlīd and that the overwhelming majority of persons under religious obligation fall within the sphere of taqlīd, whether taqlīd of the fatwa, taqlīd of the evidence, or both together. From the standpoint of the followers themselves, the extent of taqlīd may therefore resemble very closely the extent of taqlīd in the Sharīʿah. From the standpoint of theological questions themselves rather than the people following an authority, however, the scope becomes clear through the following general account.

Taqlīd is impermissible in the three principal headings—divine unity, prophethood, and the Return—whether the person under religious obligation is capable of technical ijtihād or not. [[S133-1]] Any commitment to these headings reached by a route other than independent inquiry is therefore certainly invalid, even if the proposition itself is true. What is required is not merely the proposition, even if adopted through taqlīd, but the evidence itself. The evidence need not be theoretical: innate disposition is sufficient evidence where the person attends consciously to it in a manner that produces settled conviction of the heart.

In all theological questions other than these three headings, taqlīd is permissible subject to two important considerations.

First: progression through the levels of taqlīd is necessary. The person under religious obligation begins with taqlīd of the evidence; if he is unable to do that, he may move to taqlīd of the fatwa.

Second: in certain theological questions, one may not move to taqlīd of the fatwa even where a fatwa exists in the field. In such cases and their like, however, taqlīd of the evidence remains valid.

In light of this, we can formulate a general two-part rule for identifying the nature of questions in which taqlīd is permissible and for approximating the sphere of such questions.

First: in every theological question in which the person under religious obligation is unable to exercise ijtihād, reach the evidence, and understand it, he may practice taqlīd—provided that the question is not itself one of the foundational theological principles. Taqlīd is not permissible in the very question of establishing the Necessary Existent, His ﷻ unity, or knowledge of Him ﷻ; nor is it permissible in the very question of establishing prophethood or the Return.

Second: in those theological questions where taqlīd is valid, progression is required. Whenever a person is unable to practice taqlīd of the evidence, he may practice taqlīd of the fatwa, except in questions where there is no scope for a fatwa. He may not move directly to taqlīd of the fatwa until he has exerted his capacity in attempting to understand the evidence. If he remains unable, and becomes certain—or forms an authoritative probability—that he cannot understand the evidence, he may move into the sphere of taqlīd of the fatwa. This requirement is especially emphasized for students.

The two-part rule may therefore be summarized as follows: taqlīd is permissible in every theological question where ijtihād is not possible, except the foundational headings themselves; and one may move to taqlīd of the fatwa when incapable of taqlīd of the evidence, except in questions where there is no scope for a fatwa. The details will be explained when the theological fatwas themselves are reached in another book independent of this introductory study.
